Exploding Projectile

A shell bursts in mid-air and the two pieces land at the same moment. One piece lands back at the gun. How high was the burst?

The problem

A shell leaves the ground at 400 m/s, 30° above the horizontal (take g = 10 m/s², no air resistance). It explodes in flight into two pieces. Both reach the ground at the same instant. Piece A lands exactly where the shell was fired, hitting the ground at 250 m/s.

Step 1 · Predict

Test it fires the shell and explodes it at your height on the way up. If you are right, piece A comes down on the gun.

Explore
Instructions
  1. Read the problem. Before touching the sim, work out the height of the explosion on paper.
  2. Type your height in Step 1 and press Test it. The shell flies, explodes at that height, and both pieces fall. Does piece A come down on the gun?
  3. If it misses, the message says by how far. Revise your answer and test again.
  4. Use Explore to move the explosion time, change how fast piece A is thrown backward, and change how the mass splits. Watch the grey dot, the centre of mass. What does it do?
  5. Press Show solution when you want to compare methods.
Theory

The explosion is caused by forces inside the shell. Internal forces cannot change the motion of the centre of mass. So the centre of mass keeps following the same parabola the whole shell would have followed, and it lands at the shell's original range.

The explosion also does not change the vertical velocity of either piece in this problem, because both land at the same instant as the unbroken shell would. Only the horizontal velocities change, and they change in opposite directions so that the centre of mass is not disturbed.

With these two facts, the pieces' horizontal motion after the burst is a balance: the centre of mass moves forward at v cosθ while piece A goes back at its own speed. The burst must happen when the horizontal ground covered forward by the centre of mass equals the ground piece A covers backward.

Notice that the sim lets you change the mass split. It moves where piece B lands, but not where A lands. That is why the problem never needs the masses.
