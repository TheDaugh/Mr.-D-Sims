Instructions
  1. Press Record with microphone. Your browser will ask to use the microphone. A quiet room and a hard floor work best. No microphone? Use Use simulated ball.
  2. Set Delay, the seconds between pressing the button and releasing the ball, and Record for, how long to listen. A countdown shows how long is left before recording starts.
  3. Release the ball when the countdown ends. The top graph shows the sound track, rectified and smoothed. Vertical lines show where the program thinks each bounce happened.
  4. If it has missed a bounce or found one that is not there, adjust Smoothing, Threshold and Minimum gap until the lines sit on the bounces.
  5. The lower graph shows the motion the program reconstructs from the bounce times. It assumes g = 9.81 m s−2 and no air resistance.
  6. The last few bounces are often unreliable. Set Use first N rebounds to the number you trust. The estimate of the original drop height updates at once.
Theory

Without air resistance, the height an object reaches when thrown straight up depends only on how long it stays in the air. The time to fall from the top is t = √(2h/g), from h = ½gt² with u = 0. Rising from the ground to the top takes just as long as falling back, so a full bounce between two impacts lasts 2t.

The computer records the time between impacts, Δt = 2t. So the rebound height is h = gΔt² / 8. Do this for every pair of impacts and you have the height of every rebound.

The elasticity e is the ratio of one rebound height to the one before. A perfect rebound has e = 1. In the real world e is rarely better than 0.9, even for very bouncy balls. Averaging e over several bounces gives a good value.

The first bounce is the one that happens before any timing starts, because the computer cannot hear the moment of release. Having found the height of the first rebound, h₁, divide by e to work back to the original height: H = h₁ / e. The more rebounds you record, the better the estimate. It is a curiosity rather than a precision instrument.
